# About this resource

Generative AI can participate in many parts of a social-science research process: finding sources, proposing questions, drafting analytical code, suggesting robustness checks, challenging interpretations, reorganising manuscripts and helping researchers reflect on their own workflow.

A list of possible uses tells us little about who made consequential intellectual moves, why those moves were adopted, or what evidence later justified them.

This resource therefore focuses on **research episodes** rather than capabilities.

The worked case began with a public PISA release and an existing researcher interest in Ball Mapper, topological data analysis and the interpretation of ranking systems. Generative AI entered after that substantive starting point. It accelerated some tasks, proposed a consequential question, generated analytical artefacts, challenged parts of the preferred methodological story and also produced at least one interpretation that was later withdrawn.

The resulting record provides a useful setting for asking how social scientists should document contribution and judgement when research trajectories are jointly shaped by researchers and generative systems.

## What this resource is for

Use the material to:

- discuss AI-assisted research methods with students or colleagues;
- design a documentation protocol for a new project;
- distinguish proposal generation from empirical evidence;
- document why an analytical decision changed;
- record when robustness evidence narrows a claim;
- make contribution claims more specific and auditable;
- separate contemporaneous evidence from retrospective reconstruction.

## What it is not

This resource is not:

- a claim that AI should replace researchers;
- a claim that human participation automatically guarantees quality;
- a recipe for recording every research action;
- a software repository;
- empirical validation of the reason-provenance framework;
- a substitute for subject-matter, statistical, ethical or methodological expertise.

Attribution and desirability are separate questions. A workflow can be transparently attributable without being a division of labour that a university, discipline, journal or researcher should endorse.
