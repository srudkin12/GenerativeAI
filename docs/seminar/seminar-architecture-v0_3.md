# Seminar architecture v0.3

## Seminar

**Generative AI in the Social Science Research Process: Illustrating Practice and Potential**

**Audience:** Department of Social Statistics, University of Manchester  
**Current target:** approximately 50 minutes

## Governing principle

The seminar remains conceptually anchored in the CAIS reason-provenance work, but the audience encounters the theory **after** seeing the research practice that makes the theory necessary.

The seminar should therefore feel like:

**a research story → a set of consequential episodes → a conceptual explanation → a transferable operating model**

rather than:

**a theory presentation with PISA examples added afterwards**.

# Movement 1 — Why use Generative AI in serious social-science research?
## Approx. 0–8 minutes

Establish the substantive origin of the project before introducing AI.

Core material:

- September 2026 PISA release and public debate.
- Existing researcher interest in rankings, multidimensional social outcomes and Ball Mapper/TDA.
- Initial question: can the public release support an interesting and timely social-science paper?
- Generative AI enters after the substantive opportunity is recognised.

**Key message:** The project did not begin as an AI experiment.

**Audience takeaway:** AI is most useful when attached to a real research problem rather than used as the source of the problem itself.

# Movement 2 — What did AI actually do?
## Approx. 8–26 minutes

Use contrasting episodes rather than a chronological catalogue of prompts.

### Episode A — Useful origination

AI proposes the moving-benchmark question.

**Teaching point:** AI can originate a consequential move inside a researcher-originated project.

### Episode B — Human governance

UK four-nation specification, removal of aggregate UK, re-testing radius 50, retention of the PISA 2022 caveat.

**Teaching point:** Human contribution includes constraint, refusal and specification.

### Episode C — Generated artefact versus evidence

Versioned package → local execution → handback → logs/checksums → inspection.

**Teaching point:** Generation and verification are different research functions.

### Episode D — Evidence makes the preferred method claim smaller

PCA explains approximately 97.31% of variation in the three published domain means.

**Teaching point:** A serious workflow must allow evidence to weaken the preferred methodological story.

### Episode E — Plausible number, unwarranted inference

The direct B4→B9 interpretation is withdrawn.

**Teaching point:** Correct arithmetic does not guarantee valid substantive inference.

**Audience takeaway:** Generative AI is neither merely clerical nor autonomously authoritative. Its role varies across research functions, and each function needs a different form of governance.

# Movement 3 — How do we know who or what contributed?
## Approx. 26–35 minutes

Introduce the CAIS concepts only after the audience has seen the episodes.

Main concepts:

- Origination
- Evaluation
- Integration
- Interpretation
- Attribution ambiguity
- Reason provenance

Core question:

**What evidence supports a bounded claim about how a consequential research judgement entered and changed the project?**

Explain evidence timing:

- ex-ante principles;
- contemporaneous trace;
- frozen project evidence;
- ex-post reconstruction.

Use the retrospective-storytelling episode to motivate why provenance cannot be reconstructed solely from a polished final account.

**Important boundary:** Clear attribution does not establish that a division of labour is desirable.

# Movement 4 — How do we stop the argument changing when we write it?
## Approx. 35–40 minutes

Introduce the Academic Writing Filter as manuscript governance.

### Slide 1 — Writing creates another failure point

**verified evidence → authorised claim → manuscript revision → published claim**

Warning:

**fluent rewriting can alter inferential force**

### Slide 2 — Revision under ordered constraints

**Task-relative invariants**  
evidence · inferential boundaries · qualifications · frozen distinctions

↓ constrain

**Contextual constraints**  
discipline · manuscript type · outlet/editor/referee requirements

↓ constrain

**Mutable presentation choices**  
wording · order · transitions · signposting

### PISA illustration

Use the PCA/Ball Mapper contract briefly:

- verified: PC1 ≈ 97.31%;
- authorised: PCA is the compact global summary; BM complements it for local overlap/profile relations;
- prohibited: BM reveals a fundamentally different hidden global structure;
- qualification: complement, not replacement.

Keep the detailed B4→B9 contract in backup unless rehearsal shows it is needed.

**Audience takeaway:** Generative AI may transform presentation; it must not silently transform the authorised research claim.

# Movement 5 — How would you do this yourself?
## Approx. 40–48 minutes

Present the [ten-step practical protocol](../practical-research-protocol.md).

Do not read every line. Use a single visual chain:

**problem → proposal space → specification → verification → challenge → claim change → authorisation → manuscript governance → final inspection → provenance**

Give attendees three concrete instructions:

1. **Never confuse generated artefacts with verified evidence.**
2. **Challenge the interpretation you most want to be true.**
3. **Authorise the claim before asking AI to improve the prose.**

Point to the repository as the **seminar companion and practical workbook**, not an archive of project files.

Suggested audience route:

**Quick start → ten-step protocol → worked episodes → researcher tools → workshop pathway**

# Close — What happens next?
## Approx. 48–50 minutes

Introduce the three practical workshops:

1. **Build the workflow**
2. **Challenge the research**
3. **Move from evidence to manuscript**

Each workshop produces a reusable research artefact rather than ending with discussion alone.

Final message:

> Generative AI can contribute meaningfully across the research process, but useful assistance depends on knowing which decisions matter, what counts as evidence, which claims the evidence authorises, and how those claims are preserved through to the finished scholarly object.

# Rehearsal questions

- Does the first eight minutes establish a substantive social-science problem before AI?
- Do the episodes feel genuinely different from one another?
- Does CAIS terminology arrive only after the audience can attach it to observed practice?
- Is manuscript governance clearly different from reason provenance?
- Can a participant explain the ten-step protocol after hearing it once?
- Does the GitHub resource look usable rather than like a research archive?
- Is enough time left for the final practical invitation?
