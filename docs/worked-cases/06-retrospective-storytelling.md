# Case 6 — Retrospective storytelling and the reason-provenance problem

## Research setting

Reflection on the research process raised a practical concern: researchers can tell convincing stories after the event about why they made a decision.

A polished retrospective account may be sincere and useful while still being evidentially different from a contemporaneous record.

## Consequential move

The researcher explicitly raised the problem of **retrospective storytelling** and asked how journals could distinguish among:

- ex-ante principles;
- recorded contemporaneous evidence;
- ex-post reasoning.

AI-assisted development then helped formalise the distinction. Later critical review narrowed it further by recognising that tacit or incompletely articulated judgement can still be genuine contribution.

## Outcome

The conceptual work moved away from a simple "more logging is better" position.

Later versions distinguished evidence that a person or system **changed the trajectory** from evidence about **why** that change occurred. They also focused on whether evidence discriminates the alternative contribution histories relevant to a bounded claim.

## Reason-provenance lesson

**The evidential quality of an attribution claim depends not only on what was recorded but on when it was recorded and what the record can actually establish.**

## Boundary

Retrospective reasoning is not worthless. It is a different evidential object from contemporaneous trace.

One conversational intervention also did not create the full framework. Literature, adversarial review and boundary cases materially changed the eventual theory.
